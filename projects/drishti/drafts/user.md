### Will this make my shift easier, or add work and embarrass me?

#### Before: a best-case number

Safety compliance used to be counted on announced walks, and shift in-charges warned the floor that a walk was coming [E:e02b].
Those walks said 74% of people wore both helmet and vest [M:walk_compliance].
The cameras watch every shift, nights and breaks included, and in the first two pilot weeks they found 69% [M:K1_base].
The vendor had warned about this on the very first call: "the first number you see from us will probably look worse than your walk score, not better" [E:e03].

#### The hard part: false alarms

In the proof of concept, 23% of alerts were false [E:e21].
The new Head of EHS put it plainly: "Twenty-three percent is how a system dies." [E:e22]
She set the bar: under ten percent false, every week, checked by her own team, not the vendor's [E:e23].
The vendor retrained the detector on 4,200 labelled frames from the plant's own cameras, on a box inside the plant [E:e40].
False alarms on the pilot cameras fell from 20% to under 10% by the end of the pilot [M:K4_base] [M:K4_result].
Gang leaders noticed within a day; in their words, the new version no longer raised the sack alert [E:e40b].

#### The turning point was the photo

A packing shift in-charge described arguing with contractor gang leaders about helmets: "I showed the photo. After that, no argument." [E:e24]
The Head of EHS set the rule for how it is used: "Not punishment, proof." [E:e25]
Within weeks, gang leaders were checking helmets themselves before the shift started, because nobody wanted their photo in the group [E:e45] [E:e91].
Night shift stayed weaker for longer, and the team said so openly [E:e45b].

#### A normal shift now

An alert goes as a text message to the in-charge of that area, not to a guard in the control room [E:e23] [E:e10].
If nobody acknowledges it within 5 minutes, the control room phones the in-charge [E:e50].
Acknowledgement time on the pilot cameras went from nearly 9 minutes in the first two weeks to about 2 minutes [M:K3_base] [M:K3_post_pilot].
Where people kept stepping over barriers at conveyor tail pulleys, maintenance installed physical guards, and entries then fell 77% from the start of the pilot [E:e49] [E:e57] [M:K2_post_change].
When people taking helmets off on the way to the lockers at shift change set off alerts, the rule was changed in the open and logged [E:e54].

#### What the people on the floor say

In a survey of 18 supervisors run by the client's safety team, 15 said they would recommend it [M:survey_n] [M:survey_yes].
One wrote: "Earlier I was blamed for violations I never saw; now I see them first" [E:s1].
Another: "Response time improved because control room calls directly" [E:s2].
Not everything is solved: some find the alerts at shift change too many, and some workers still see it as punishment [E:s4] [E:s3].

#### Credit where it is due

The plant's own teams did much of the work: a safety week, daily toolbox talks, and suspending two contractor gangs who were identified from the snapshots [E:e41] [E:e42].
